Baseline & tracking terms

EHS — Erection Hardness Score
What it means: EHS is a one-item clinical scale used to describe erection firmness. It runs from 1 to 4.
Your plan says: use it as an optional baseline/checkpoint metric alongside morning erection frequency, bodyweight, and waist.
Research note: EHS has been validated as a simple patient-reported outcome. If you track it, treat it as one trend, not a grade on yourself.
Research: EHS validation ↗
Morning erection frequency
What it means: simply a private yes/no observation after waking. Sleep stage, stress, medications, illness and many other factors can affect it.
Your plan says: track days per week and compare trends at baseline, Week 8 and Week 12.
Research note: one morning is noisy data. Look at longer trends rather than interpreting a single day.
Bodyweight, waist & photos
What it means: bodyweight is total scale weight; waist is a simple body-composition trend marker. Measure your waist in the same place, under similar conditions, each time.
Your plan says: use bodyweight and waist at baseline and checkpoints. Photos stay outside the app.

Pelvic floor: Kegels & relaxation

What is the pelvic floor?
A group of muscles at the bottom of the pelvis that help support the bladder and bowel and help with urinary, bowel and sexual function.
Kegels — beginner definition
What it means: a Kegel is a small contraction of the pelvic-floor muscles. Think of the muscles you would use to stop urine or gas — but do not make a habit of training while urinating.
Your plan says, Weeks 1–4: 3 sessions/day. Each session: 10 slow contractions held 5 seconds, 10 quick contractions, then 10 relaxation/reverse-Kegel reps.
Your plan says, Weeks 5–12: build the slow holds to 8–10 seconds while breathing normally, keep 3 sessions/day, use more than one position, and do not keep escalating volume.
Research note: Cleveland Clinic recommends focusing on correct technique and warns that overdoing Kegels can increase pelvic-floor tension or worsen symptoms. Stop if you get pain or urinary symptoms.
Cleveland Clinic: Kegels for men ↗ Video: beginner Kegel guide by pelvic-floor physiotherapist Michelle Kenway ↗
Reverse Kegel / pelvic-floor relaxation
What it means: the relaxation side of pelvic-floor control. Instead of squeezing, you practice letting the pelvic-floor muscles soften and lengthen while breathing normally.
Your plan says: include relaxation every session so the routine is not only tightening.
Research note: if your pelvic floor is already overactive or painful, more strengthening can make things worse; a pelvic-floor physical therapist can help tell the difference.

Cardio & strength

Zone 2 cardio
What it means: comfortable aerobic work. A common rough estimate is about 60–70% of estimated max heart rate, but the easiest beginner check is that you can still hold a light conversation.
Your plan says, Weeks 1–4: 30–40 minutes, 4 times per week. Examples: brisk incline walk, easy bike or easy jog.
Your plan says, Weeks 5–12: progress to 45–50 minutes, 4–5 times per week.
Cleveland Clinic: Zone 2 explained ↗
HIIT — 8 × 30 sec hard / 90 sec easy
What it means: high-intensity interval training. One round is 30 seconds of hard effort followed by 90 seconds of easy recovery.
Your plan says: add one HIIT session per week in Phase 2. It is intentionally much shorter than Zone 2 work.
Strength training / compound lifts
What it means: exercises that train several muscle groups at once. Examples include squat patterns, hip hinges/deadlifts, presses, rows and lunges. “Legs included” means lower-body work is not optional.
Your plan says: strength train 3 times per week.

Sleep, food & supplements

Sleep & fixed wake time
Your plan says: 7–9 hours nightly and a consistent wake time.
Research note: for teenagers ages 13–18, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8–10 hours per 24 hours. So if that age range applies, use 8–10 as the stronger general-health target.
AASM sleep-duration consensus ↗
Protein — 1.6 g/kg
What it means: multiply bodyweight in kilograms by 1.6. If you use pounds first, divide pounds by 2.205 to get kilograms.
Your plan says: use 1.6 g/kg/day in Phase 2+.
Research note: protein needs in adolescents depend on growth, total calories and training load. The app keeps 1.6 g/kg as your plan target, not as a universal prescription.
Beetroot, leafy greens & nitric oxide
What it means: beetroot and leafy greens contain dietary nitrate, which can be converted into nitric oxide in the body.
Your plan says: 500 mL beetroot juice or a concentrate shot, plus a handful of rocket/arugula or spinach.
Research note: beetroot/nitrate research shows possible exercise effects, but results and dosing vary. Food sources are the lower-risk way to approach nitrate intake.
Citrulline
What it means: an amino acid the body can convert into arginine and then nitric oxide.
Your plan says: 1.5–3 g/day.
Research note: NIH says evidence for performance benefit is limited and longer-term supplement safety is not well established. The tracker treats this as a plan reference, not a recommendation.
NIH: exercise supplements fact sheet ↗
Supplement tiers from your plan
Your plan listed as stronger-evidence references: creatine, caffeine, vitamin D when appropriate, omega-3, magnesium, zinc when diet is poor, citrulline and beetroot.
Your plan listed as weaker-evidence: maca, tongkat ali, ashwagandha and fenugreek.
Research note: for young athletes, food-first guidance is preferred and supplement decisions should be individualized. Products can also be contaminated or mislabeled.

Grooming & skin care

Trim vs shave
Your plan says: trim around 6 mm weekly rather than fully shaving.
Research note: shaving can cause ingrown hairs and irritation. If you do shave, use a sharp clean razor, lubrication and shave with hair growth.
Cleveland Clinic: ingrown pubic hair prevention ↗
Moisturize, exfoliate & hygiene
Your plan says: use an unscented moisturizer after showering, exfoliate gently once weekly, and do a thorough wash/dry weekly.
Beginner rule: external skin only, gentle products, no fragrance if it irritates you, and stop any product that burns or causes a rash.
Foreskin hygiene: if you have a foreskin that retracts comfortably, rinse underneath gently and return it to its normal position. Never force a tight or painful foreskin.
NHS: cleaning under the foreskin / phimosis guidance ↗
Body fat, waist & visible length
Your plan says: reducing excess suprapubic fat can make more of the shaft externally visible and uses waist as the honest tracker.
Research note: suprapubic fat can reduce apparent visible length, but a precise promise like “5% fat loss reveals 1–1.5 cm” is not a reliable universal rule. Track waist/body composition, not a guaranteed centimeter outcome.

Phase checkpoints & mindset

Week 8 checkpoint
Your plan says: compare morning erection frequency, optional EHS, waist, bodyweight, sleep/cardio consistency and your overall wellbeing with Week 1.
Weeks 11–12: stop obsessing over the score
Your plan says: keep the healthy habits but reduce obsessive self-monitoring. The point of 8+ weeks of tracking is to give you enough trend data that you no longer need to judge every single day.
Confidence
Use the evidence you generated yourself: sleep consistency, fitness, waist/body composition trends, training adherence, grooming and general wellbeing. The plan’s core mindset line is: “Nobody’s counting.”
Communication and paying attention to another person beats mind-reading and self-judging. The tracker should support confidence, not become another source of anxiety.

Medication & safety review

Medication / habit review
Your plan says: if relevant, review medicines or habits that can affect energy, sexual function or training — including finasteride, SSRIs, beta blockers, isotretinoin, nicotine, heavy cannabis use or regular alcohol.
Important: do not stop a prescription medicine on your own. This is a clinician conversation.
Do not do
Avoid jelqing, clamping, hanging weights, unsupervised enlargement devices, pumps used as a size hack, high-dose yohimbine, unregulated “male enhancement/size” products and testosterone boosters without medical indication. Pain is a stop signal, not a training goal.
